# Story types

Word counts are defaults. House style or the user's request overrides them.

## Brief (75-250 words)

Lede, two or three supporting facts, then what happens next. No nut graf needed. One quote at most.

## Breaking news (300-600 words)

- Lead with what's confirmed. Keep confirmed facts apart from early reports, and attribute each one: "according to the sheriff's office."
- Put the time of the last update at the top or bottom as house style directs. When updating, rewrite the lede rather than adding paragraphs to the bottom.
- Don't use casualty numbers, names, or causes until an official source confirms them. Use `[NEED: ...]` for whatever is still pending.

## Meeting story (400-700 words)

- Lead with the decision and what it does to residents: cost, schedule, rules, services. Don't lead with the meeting itself. "Willard residents will see higher water bills next year after ..." beats "Willard City Council met Monday."
- Give the vote count and who voted no (`approved 5-2, with Smith and Doe voting no`) and why, if they said.
- Translate money into reader terms: a total, per household, or per $100,000 of home value.
- Say what happens next: second reading, effective date, next meeting.
- Give one story to each major decision. Cut unrelated "in other business" items, or move them to a separate list of briefs.
- Identify public commenters by full name and where they live, as they gave it.

## Levy or election story (400-800 words)

- For a levy, say whether it's a renewal, a replacement, or a new (additional) levy, plus its millage and how many years it runs. Say what it pays for and what happens if it fails.
- Give the cost per $100,000 of home value, using the county auditor's estimate. Attribute that figure, and never calculate it yourself.
- Quote the ballot language only where its exact wording matters. Otherwise, explain it in plain words.
- Give candidates in a race equal treatment in length and placement. Report results with the vote counts and percentages, and say whether they're unofficial.

## Police and courts (100-500 words)

- Attribute everything to police, court records, or prosecutors. Report the specific charge: "charged with felonious assault," not "charged in an attack."
- "Arrested" and "charged" are different steps. Use "alleged" sparingly and attach it to the act ("the alleged theft"), never to the person.
- Write "homicide" or "killing" until there's a murder conviction. Write "crash," not "accident." Use "pleaded not guilty."
- Follow house policy on naming suspects, juveniles, and victims, and on mug shots.
- If you reported a charge, report how it ends: conviction, acquittal, or dismissal.
- Flag every story that accuses a named person of a crime as `LEGAL_RISK` for editor review.

## Obituary (300-600 words)

A news obituary is different from a paid death notice. Give the full name, age, hometown, date of death, and cause only if the family or an official source discloses it. Follow with what the person did and why they mattered locally, then survivors and service details. Use quotes from family, colleagues, or friends. Courtesy titles follow house style.

## Feature or profile (800-1,500 words)

- A scene or anecdote lede is allowed, but it must connect to a nut graf by paragraph 5.
- Show the subject through specific details, actions, and their own words, not adjectives.
- Organize by theme or by a timeline that serves the reader. A kicker ending (a closing quote or scene) is allowed.
- Every detail still needs a source. Don't invent scenes, thoughts, or feelings.

## Explainer (600-1,200 words)

Label it an explainer if house style requires it. Organize it around the questions readers are asking ("What is the levy for?" "How much will I pay?"), with plain-language answers. Mark what is known, what isn't known yet, and when it will be.

## Press-release rewrite (100-400 words)

Find the actual news, which is often buried in the third paragraph. Cut the puffery, superlatives, and quotes that say nothing. Verify the claims that can be checked, and attribute the rest: "according to a news release." Add context or an outside voice where you can. Never run a release as it was sent.
