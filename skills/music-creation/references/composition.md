# Composition and arrangement

Start with the existing melody, chords, beat, performer, or instrumental identity, and leave unknowns open rather than inventing precision. Describe reference music through audible traits, and separate observed features of supplied material from guesses based on a genre or artist name.

Don't invent a singer's comfortable range. When exact melody is requested, supply unambiguous pitches and rhythm in notation or an event list; contour prose is only a sketch. State the tonal context for Roman numerals, and check actual melody notes against the chords when notes exist. Familiar progressions can be right, and a key change is optional.

A chorus can arrive through space or a lower register; it need not be louder or higher. Repeated grooves, static textures, and identical choruses may be central to the style.

For substantial arrangements, use a compact section table: length if known, musical purpose, lead and supporting parts, and transition. If exact duration and bars are specified, reconcile them: seconds = bars × quarter-note beats per bar × 60 / quarter-note BPM at constant meter and tempo. Account separately for tempo changes, pickups, and free time.

State intended mix outcomes when useful for handoff; actual mixing, mastering, and audio processing are outside this skill.
