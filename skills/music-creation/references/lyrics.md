# Lyrics, rhyme, and revision

## Find the center

Turn a broad topic into an angle: what does the speaker admit, refuse, request, remember, or avoid saying? Establish a situation when useful. Atmospheric or declarative work can use a recurring phrase, sound, or attitude instead of a photographable object.

Choose the voice: private thought, direct address, storyteller, collective, or another intentional position. Track pronoun references and signal meaningful changes of time or perspective.

When stuck, collect sensory material before polishing lines. Consider sight, sound, touch, smell, taste, bodily sensation, and motion. Select details that share a world or develop an image; unrelated vivid nouns do not create coherence. For coaching, offer one useful exercise or question. When asked to draft, write rather than imposing lessons.

## Shape sections

Choose a form the material supports: verse/chorus, verse/refrain, AABA, strophic, through-composed, or custom. A bridge should earn its space through a new angle or musical departure.

Verses often develop detail while a chorus concentrates the emotional center. Explain how later verses change understanding: events, pressure, perspective, or deeper detail can all do this. Place the hook where its words and music gain emphasis. Keep repeated choruses when repetition strengthens them; suggest a final variation only when it adds something. Shared verse/chorus imagery is not automatically a fault.

## Check sound and fit

For English, distinguish lexical stress from sentence emphasis. Compare corresponding phrases for stress placement, pickups, durations, rests, and breathing. Equal syllable counts can conceal incompatible stresses. Do not infer exact bars from stress counts alone.

For fixed melodies, use supplied notation, timing, or the musician's phrasing as authority. Show the relevant stressed slots and preserve locked words or notes. Without a melody, offer text-level scansion and identify what remains to be sung or auditioned.

Check consonant congestion at speed, sustained vowels, and breaths that interrupt grammar. Match density to tempo, delivery, and phrase duration; avoid universal line, syllable, or word limits. Rap, melisma, chants, and sparse ballads need different treatment.

Use the requested language and dialect. Do not export English stress rules to every language: tonal-language lyrics may need tone-melody checks, and Japanese phrasing may require mora awareness. Disclose uncertainty in pronunciation or language-specific judgments.

## Choose rhyme by sound and purpose

Build vocabulary from the song's subject. Offer relevant exact rhymes, near rhymes, assonance, internal rhymes, or multiword rhymes as useful. Judge sound in the intended pronunciation, not spelling. Preserve natural grammar and meaning. A repeated word is repetition rather than a new rhyme; either can be intentional.

Use rhyme placement and strength to influence expectation and closure. Exact rhyme may feel settled, while looser rhyme can leave motion open; neither guarantees an emotion. Unrhymed writing is legitimate. Do not force a rhyme break merely to satisfy an audit.

## Review and revise

Locate the obstacle to the intended effect. Quote a short line or name the section, explain its likely listener effect, and suggest a focused remedy. Distinguish fit problems from taste.

When revision is requested, repair meaning and structure, unnecessary words, then phrasing as needed. Recheck neighboring lines after rhyme or stress changes. Preserve register, humor, ambiguity, roughness, and the chosen ending. Offer alternatives for real creative forks, not cosmetic synonym lists.

Symmetry, abstract words, and familiar rhymes are prompts for examination, not evidence of authorship. Do not claim AI detection or plagiarism clearance.
